FaceJev: Direct Visual Decisions for Face Forgery Detection

Paper arXiv cs.CV (recent)

Face forgery detection methods often perform well on seen manipulations but generalize poorly to unseen ones. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) can recognize facial parts and assess their visual plausibility. Existing MLLM-based methods typically express these judgments through textual explanations, but seldom treat individual part decisions as explicit learning targets. We present FaceJev, an MLLM-based method for face forgery detection through direct visual decisions. Following the Jev formulation, FaceJev answers eight independent questions about facial parts using a shared image encoding and reads anomaly probabilities from the native answer logits. A face is classified as forged if any part is predicted to be abnormal, directly linking the detection result to the part decisions. We construct PartForensics, a dataset of approximately 160k images with binary anomaly targets for the eight facial parts, to supervise these decisions. We further propose FS-GRPO, which optimizes each part decision through an exact reward comparison with its alternative, using both detection and part rewards. FaceJev outperforms the strongest baseline by 7.25 percentage points on unseen manipulations. Extensive ablation studies validate our design choices and the effectiveness of the main components.

How VLMs Plan What's Next

Paper arXiv cs.CV (recent)

When a vision-language model describes an image, it names one object at a time and it rarely repeats itself, yet how the model plans this order is far from obvious. We characterize the mechanism a VLM uses to decide where to look before it looks. The mechanism has four main parts. First, binding heads match the relevant text tokens against the image and resolve them into pointers to the matched image region. Second, the same binding heads bind each written answer to the region it came from, and the model turns it into a progress signal: a cursor, the position in the image up to which the list has been written. Third, a select step chooses the nearest unwritten region after the cursor, or stops. Finally, gaze heads execute the chosen pointer and return a concept of what is drawn there, which language heads and the late MLPs turn into a word in the language of the question. We validate each part of this mechanism through causal patching in Qwen3-VL models on a synthetic visual retrieval task, and test our mechanisms against alternative hypotheses. We further show that the mechanism holds across input formats, natural photographs, and languages, and that its binding, relay, and gaze heads appear at every model size from 2B to 32B. This work allows us to investigate how a VLM turns a question and an image into a sequence of answers, layer by layer and token by token. More broadly, it opens a way to study how models plan over complex multi-step tasks with long rollouts. Our project is available at https://plan.baulab.info/

What Does CLIP Learn for Regional Geolocalization? Probing Visual Cues and Scene Configuration After Adaptation

Paper arXiv cs.CV (recent)

Large collections of street-view imagery provide rich visual information about urban environments, but extracting fine-grained geographic information from such data remains challenging. In particular, fine-grained regional geolocalization is challenging because nearby areas often share coarse geographic cues. We study regional geolocalization within a metropolitan area and ask whether pretrained CLIP features are sufficient for regional discrimination, and what visual information supports performance after adaptation. Using 9,085 street-view images from eight Greater Los Angeles regions, we compare zero-shot CLIP, frozen-encoder readouts, partial encoder updating, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), and full fine-tuning. Frozen readouts remain near the 39.03% zero-shot accuracy, whereas encoder adaptation achieves 75.94-82.10%. Full fine-tuning also reduces the mean distance to the predicted region center from 12.30 km to 3.86 km. We probe these gains through semantic cue removal, appearance reduction using edge maps and blur, and scene-configuration disruption using patch scrambling. Adapted models achieve higher edge and blur accuracy and switch 42.92-45.56% of predictions after scrambling, compared with 10.79-14.60% for frozen methods. However, adaptation does not improve the fraction of performance retained after appearance reduction, while vegetation and sky remain influential. A Caltech101 control further shows that scrambling sensitivity is not unique to geolocalization. Overall, encoder adaptation substantially improves nearby-region discrimination and is associated with greater sensitivity to intact scene configuration, without evidence that coarse structure alone becomes sufficient for prediction. These conclusions concern viewpoint variation near known locations rather than geographically disjoint generalization.

ViRA: Toward Ultra-High-Resolution Visual Agents via Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization

Paper arXiv cs.CV (recent)

Visual agents answer questions about ultra-high-resolution photos by zooming into the relevant regions. However, existing agents often answer from common sense instead of reading the evidence. Reinforcement learning (RL) on the final answer cannot correct this, because a guess earns the same reward as reading. We propose Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization (CrossRPO), which trains agents on contrast sets. A contrast set asks one question about several versions of an image that differ only in a small region with the evidence and have different answers, so only reading the evidence solves the whole set. CrossRPO rewards solving the whole set, and we prove that it does not reinforce a set answered from common sense. We build contrast sets for training (ViRA-Train) and evaluation (ViRA-Bench) by editing one small object in images of 21 to 120 megapixels, and we photograph further sets with real changes (ViRA-Bench-Real) to exclude editing traces. We train two Visual Reasoning Agents, ViRA-9B and ViRA-27B, with CrossRPO on only about 3,500 items of ViRA-Train each. CrossRPO achieves 63.3% set accuracy on ViRA-Bench at the maximum resolution, against 59.3% for GRPO. ViRA-9B is the most accurate open model of its size on all benchmarks, and ViRA-27B outperforms Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6-Luna, both at their highest thinking level, on most benchmarks.

A Systematic Analysis of Out-of-Distribution Detection Under Representation and Training Paradigm Shifts

Paper arXiv cs.CV (recent)

We present a systematic benchmark of out-of-distribution (OOD) detection confidence scoring functions (CSFs) through a representation-centric lens. Our study spans CNN and ViT backbones, multiple training paradigms, four image-classification source datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SuperCIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet), and OOD datasets grouped into near, mid, and far regimes based on CLIP-derived semantic distances. To compare CSFs across these settings, we employ a rank-based pipeline of Friedman and Conover tests that identifies top cliques of statistically indistinguishable winners under threshold-free ranking metrics (AURC and AUGRC). The main empirical finding is that the family of CSFs that are competitive detectors correlates with characteristics of the learned representation. For both CNNs and ViTs, simple probabilistic scores dominate misclassification detection. On CNNs, margin-based scores are strongest in near-OOD regimes, while geometry-aware scores such as NNGuide, fDBD, and CTM become more competitive as shift severity increases. On fine-tuned ViTs, the top cliques are led mainly by reconstruction- and residual-based scores. To interpret these ranking shifts, we analyze the last-layer representation using Neural Collapse (NC) metrics. The resulting picture is consistent across architectures: prototype- and boundary-aware scores become stronger when the representation is more collapsed and better aligned with classifier weights, whereas weaker-collapse regimes favor gradient- and manifold-based scores. Building on these insights, we propose two contributions: a simple PCA-based projection-filtering procedure that improves detector performance, and an approach that uses only the NC measurements of a trained classifier to predict its competitive out-of-distribution detector shortlist, without OOD data for that classifier and without knowledge of the shift.
